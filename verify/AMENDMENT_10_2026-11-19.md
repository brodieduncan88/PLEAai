# Amendment 10, 19 November 2026

Private working material. Times are UTC.

## What it repairs

A record could not show that it was the latest. An older record leaves out later runs and still verifies. This is D7 in the
defect register, the reviewer's point 3 of 17 November in its first half, and release gate G6, "witness-signed freshness
checkpoints". `DECLARED_AMEND10.md` states the changes, the expected outcomes, the development work and what stays open.

In short:

- **The witness signs a checkpoint** of a baseline's chain on request: how many decisions it holds, the hash of the last, the
  budget's state and the witness's clock, with the asker's nonce signed back. On request it also gives the head after an
  earlier decision. Reading it adds no decision. A client certificate given only `--tls-allow-read` may read checkpoints and
  nothing else.
- **A record carries one.** The checker's ninth check requires it to be the witness's, for this budget, and to name the
  record's own last decision and head. A record without one verifies as before and says it has none.
- **The checker says whether a record is current**, against a checkpoint obtained separately. It prints CURRENT, SUPERSEDED,
  SUPERSEDED_UNPROVEN, FORKED, OLDER, STALE or INVALID, and exits 0 only for CURRENT. It allows an hour unless told otherwise,
  and up to five minutes of clock difference.
- **A second party can keep the checkpoints.** `verify/checkpoints.py` fetches them with a nonce of its own and ties each to
  the last one kept, by the witness's own signature. It refuses a rollback or a fork, and puts the checkpoint that shows it
  aside as evidence.

## What changed on the way

The code went through four forms before the declaration.

Two independent reviews were made. The first, of the first form, reported nine findings of medium weight or more. The most
serious were these:

- the keeper's certificate could write to the witness;
- an old checkpoint read CURRENT when no age was given;
- a checkpoint dated in the future never went stale;
- the keeper's log did not tie one checkpoint to the next, so it missed a rewritten chain, and it threw away the checkpoint
  that showed a fork.

The second review, of those fixes, reported four. The most serious, of high weight, was that the keeper did not check that
the witness's answer named the sequence number it had asked about. So a witness that had rewritten its chain could still be
kept.

Each finding was fixed or is stated as open, and the declaration lists them one by one. R25 grew from 13 checks to 19 to test
them.

A pass of 50 deliberate defects on the third form caught 44. Six stopped R25 with an error before its checks could be read.
R25 was changed so that each such step gives an answer and a check fails. On the fourth form both suites passed in
development with the counts declared, and the pass of deliberate defects caught all 50, with the control passing. The third
and fourth forms were not themselves read by a reviewer.

## What stays open

The declaration's "What stays open" section is the full list. The main items are:

- CURRENT says only that a checkpoint within the age allowed ends where the record does. An old checkpoint handed over again
  with an old record reads CURRENT within that age. Only a checkpoint that the reader fetched with a nonce of their own shows
  that the record is current now.
- The witness is still one party. A witness that tells everyone the same false chain is not caught. The keeper catches a
  witness that tells it two different things.
- In the demonstration the keeper's log is on the same machine and account as everything else. The checkpoint's time is the
  witness's clock.
- A notice is still a file the service writes about itself. G5, an independent notification path, is the next gate.
